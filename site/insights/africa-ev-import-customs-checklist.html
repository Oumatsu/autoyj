The Complete Document Checklist for Importing a Chinese EV Into Africa

African customs clearance for imported vehicles has always been document-intensive.

autoYJ Intelligence
February 20, 2026
7 min

African customs clearance for imported vehicles has always been document-intensive. Chinese EVs add two new layers of complexity: battery-specific technical requirements that existing ICE vehicle documentation frameworks were not designed for, and the new OEM documentation requirements introduced by China's January 2026 export regulations.

This checklist is based on the documented requirements of the four largest African EV import markets by volume: Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana. Country-specific variations are noted where they apply. For any individual shipment, work with a licensed customs clearing agent in the destination country — this guide provides the framework, not the legal instrument.

Part 1: China-Side Export Documents

These documents are generated before the vehicle leaves China. Missing any of them creates a problem that cannot be resolved at the African port.

1. Bill of Lading (BL)

The fundamental ocean freight document. For RoRo shipments, this is typically a RoRo Bill of Lading issued by the shipping line. For container shipments, a standard container BL applies.

The BL must state:

Consignee name and address (your entity in the destination country)

Vessel name and voyage number

Port of loading and port of discharge

Vehicle description (make, model, year)

Container or RoRo slot number

Freight terms (FOB, CIF, etc.)

2. Commercial Invoice

Issued by the seller. Must show:

Seller and buyer details

VIN number

Vehicle description (make, model, year, engine/motor type)

Transaction price in USD (or agreed currency)

Payment terms

3. Packing List

A supporting document confirming the vehicle contents and accessories. For EVs, should explicitly list the charging cable type(s) included.

Physical inspection at Djibouti or Mombasa is routine. Vehicles selected for examination are checked against documentation — VIN plate, engine number (or motor serial number for EVs), and general condition. Discrepancies between document descriptions and physical vehicle generate holds.

4. Certificate of Origin (COO)

Certifies that the vehicle was manufactured in China. Issued by the China Council for the Promotion of International Trade (CCPIT) or the relevant Chinese Chamber of Commerce.

Important for African markets: COO determines which trade agreement duty rates apply. Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana are all members of COMESA or ECOWAS, but these regional agreements do not extend preferences to Chinese imports. Standard MFN duty rates apply in all four markets unless a bilateral arrangement applies.

5. Vehicle Inspection Certificate / Pre-Shipment Inspection Report (PSI)

Several African countries (including Ethiopia and Nigeria) require a Pre-Shipment Inspection certificate from an approved inspection company (Bureau Veritas, SGS, Intertek) confirming the vehicle matches the stated specifications, VIN, and condition.

For Ethiopia: QSAE (Quality and Standards Authority of Ethiopia) has approved several inspection entities. Confirm current approved inspectors before shipment.

6. OEM After-Sales Service Confirmation Document

This document must:

Be issued by the vehicle manufacturer (not a dealer or trader)

Carry the manufacturer's official seal (公章)

Specify the destination country

Reference the VIN number

If your vehicle was manufactured or first registered in 2025 or 2026 and was exported within 180 days of its registration date, absence of this document means the vehicle cannot legally leave China. Any supplier who cannot provide this document for a recently-manufactured vehicle is a compliance red flag.

Part 2: Technical Documents for EVs

Standard ICE vehicle imports require an engine number. EVs require a different set of technical identifiers.

7. Battery Specification Sheet

A technical document from the battery manufacturer (CATL, BYD, SVOLT, or the OEM) specifying:

Battery chemistry (LFP, NCM, sodium-ion)

Nominal capacity (kWh)

Voltage

UN number for dangerous goods classification

Battery serial number

Many African customs authorities now require UN38.3 dangerous goods transport documentation for EV battery shipments. This is increasingly enforced at Djibouti and Mombasa.

8. UN38.3 Test Summary (Dangerous Goods Transport Certificate)

EV batteries are classified as dangerous goods under international transport regulations. The UN38.3 test summary is the document proving the battery has been certified for safe transport.

For Chinese EV brands, this document should be available from the OEM or battery supplier. It is not commonly required for RoRo ocean freight of assembled vehicles (as distinct from batteries shipped separately), but port authorities in Kenya and Ethiopia have occasionally requested it for physical inspection lots.

9. EV Homologation or Type Approval Documentation

Several African markets are moving toward requiring evidence that imported EVs meet local or international safety standards. This is currently mandatory in South Africa (SABS approval) and increasingly requested (though not always mandatory) in Kenya and Ethiopia.

For vehicles without home-country type approval documentation, importers sometimes use an independent inspection certificate as a substitute. Confirm the current requirements with a licensed clearing agent before finalising the shipment.

Part 3: Destination-Specific Additional Requirements

Ethiopia

ERCA Import Declaration (Customs Form): filed at the port of entry (typically Djibouti, cleared by Ethiopian customs liaison officers stationed there)

Letter of Credit or Bank Transfer Proof: ERCA requires evidence of payment channel (wire transfer through licensed Ethiopian bank or an approved forex mechanism)

QSAE Pre-Shipment Inspection Report: from an approved inspector (see above)

Kenya

Kenya Revenue Authority Single Customs Territory (SCT) declaration: applies to vehicles entering through EAC partner state ports (Djibouti, Dar es Salaam)

Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) Permit: required for vehicles that have not previously been approved under a recognised international type approval scheme

Age restriction documentation: Kenya restricts import of vehicles over 8 years old; for Chinese EVs, manufacture date confirmation is required

Nigeria

NAFDAC / SON permits for EV batteries: Nigeria Standards Organisation (SON) has developed inspection protocols for imported EV batteries; requirements were expanding in 2025

Form M (Import licence): issued by Nigerian banks; required before goods can be shipped

Combined Certificate of Value and Origin (CCVO): required for Nigeria customs valuation

Ghana

Customs Declaration (CD): filed through Ghana Revenue Authority's Integrated Customs Management System (ICUMS)

Road Traffic Authority (DVLA) vehicle inspection: for used and reconditioned imports; new vehicles may be exempt

2024 zero-tariff eligibility documentation: since Ghana's 2024 automotive import tariff revision, EVs meeting specific technical criteria qualify for zero import duty — documentation of those criteria is required to claim the exemption
