## Overview

EV battery degradation — the gradual loss of a battery pack's ability to hold its original charge — is the single most common concern customers raise before buying an electric vehicle in Africa. Most of the time, dealers answer this question poorly: either dismissing it ("the battery is fine, don't worry") or not knowing enough to give a credible response. This guide explains how lithium battery degradation actually works, what accelerates it in African operating conditions, what customers should realistically expect, and how to use this knowledge to close deals rather than lose them.

---

## What Degradation Actually Is

A lithium-ion battery pack contains hundreds or thousands of individual cells. Each cell stores energy through a reversible chemical reaction between a lithium-containing cathode and an anode (typically graphite). Over time and repeated charge/discharge cycles, this reaction becomes slightly less efficient — a small amount of lithium is permanently consumed in side reactions rather than being stored usefully. The result is a gradual reduction in the amount of energy the pack can hold. This is capacity fade.

**The key number to know:** Most lithium battery packs (including BYD Blade Battery and the NMC/LFP chemistry in MG, Chery, and other Chinese EVs) retain 70–80% of their original capacity after 1,500–2,000 full charge cycles, according to battery industry benchmarks.

**What this means in practice:**
- A 400 km rated range vehicle loses roughly 20–30% capacity after 1,500 cycles
- At that point, range is 280–320 km — still usable for most daily applications
- At a typical daily charge of 50 km, 1,500 cycles = approximately 75,000 km of driving
- At taxi-level usage (200 km/day), 1,500 cycles = approximately 300,000 km

The battery is not going to "die" within the typical ownership period of a personal vehicle.

## The Four Factors That Accelerate Degradation

Not all batteries age at the same rate. Four factors determine how fast a specific vehicle's battery will degrade — and three of them are directly controllable by the owner.

### Factor 1: Heat (the biggest accelerant)

High temperature is lithium battery chemistry's primary enemy. At elevated temperature, the electrolyte inside each cell degrades faster, and a resistive film (called the SEI layer) builds up on the anode more rapidly — permanently consuming lithium that can no longer participate in energy storage.

**African relevance:** This is a significant concern for vehicles operating in Ethiopia's lowland regions (Dire Dawa, Afar zone, the Rift Valley floor) where ambient temperatures regularly exceed 35°C. For vehicles parked in direct sun with no shade — a black-roofed car can reach 60–70°C interior temperatures — passive battery heating is a genuine degradation accelerant over a multi-year ownership period.

**What dealers should say:** "If your vehicle will be parked outdoors in a hot region, try to park in shade wherever possible. This is simple to do and it meaningfully extends battery life."

### Factor 2: Charging to 100% and discharging to 0% repeatedly

Lithium cells experience the most electrochemical stress at the extremes of their state of charge (SOC) range. Charging to 100% and leaving the pack at 100% for extended periods accelerates SEI layer formation. Running the pack to near-zero repeatedly causes similar stress at the other end.

**The widely used recommendation:** For daily use, charge to 80–90% and avoid running below 15–20% regularly. Reserve 100% charge for long trips where full range is needed.

Most Chinese EVs sold in Africa allow the driver to set a charge limit (typically accessible through the infotainment system). Show customers how to set this at handover.

### Factor 3: Repeated fast charging

DC fast charging delivers energy to the battery at high current. High current = more heat generated within the cells. More heat = faster degradation. Occasional fast charging causes minimal long-term impact; daily reliance on fast charging is a meaningful degradation accelerant over 3–5 years.

**African relevance:** Taxi fleet operators who fast-charge multiple times per day will see faster degradation than personal use owners. This should be factored into fleet TCO calculations and realistic conversations with fleet buyers.

### Factor 4: Calendar ageing (time itself)

Even a battery that is never used degrades over time. The electrolyte slowly deteriorates at a chemical level regardless of cycling. This effect is minor over typical ownership periods (3–7 years) but becomes more significant for vehicles stored for 12+ months.

## LFP vs NMC: Why Chemistry Matters for African Dealers

Chinese EVs sold in Africa use two main battery chemistries, and they behave differently in terms of degradation:

**LFP (Lithium Iron Phosphate) — BYD Blade Battery, some entry-level models:**
- Lower peak energy density (shorter range per kg of battery)
- Significantly more thermally stable — less sensitive to heat degradation
- Can be charged to 100% regularly with minimal penalty
- Better suited to African climate conditions
- Typically retains capacity better over high cycle counts

**NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt) — many MG, Chery, and other Chinese EV models:**
- Higher energy density (more range for the same battery weight)
- More sensitive to heat and to charging above 90% regularly
- Better suited to moderate climate, lower mileage use cases

**For dealers:** When a customer asks "which battery is better for hot conditions?", the honest answer is LFP. This is a genuine product knowledge differentiator and customers who understand it become more confident buyers.

## What Customers Will Experience Over Time

Here is a realistic timeline for a personal vehicle owner in an Ethiopian urban context (60 km/day, overnight AC charging, 80% charge limit):

| Years of ownership | Approximate capacity retained | Real-world range (from 400 km rated) |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1–2 | ~98% | ~390 km |
| Year 3–4 | ~92% | ~368 km |
| Year 5–6 | ~85% | ~340 km |
| Year 7–8 | ~78% | ~312 km |
| Year 10 | ~70% | ~280 km |

*Editorial estimate based on LFP chemistry benchmarks and typical urban usage. Actual results vary by model, charging practice, and climate.*

For a customer driving 60 km/day in Addis, even at year 10 the vehicle has 280 km of real-world range — more than sufficient. The battery is not the vehicle's limiting factor within any normal ownership horizon.

## Using This Knowledge in a Sales Conversation

When a customer says "I'm worried the battery will wear out" — this is your moment to build trust with knowledge, not dismiss the concern.

**Effective response structure:**
1. Acknowledge the concern is reasonable: "That's the right question to ask."
2. Explain the mechanism briefly: "Batteries do lose a small amount of capacity over time — around 2–3% per year in normal use."
3. Anchor to real numbers: "After 8 years of daily use in Addis, you'd expect to have around 85% of the original range — which on this vehicle is still [X] km. For your commute of [Y] km, that's still well within range."
4. Name the controllable factors: "The main things that protect battery life are: avoid charging to 100% every day, and park in shade in hot weather. That's it."
5. Close on confidence: "Customers who understand how to charge their EV correctly get the best life out of the battery. I'll show you the charge limit setting before you drive away."
