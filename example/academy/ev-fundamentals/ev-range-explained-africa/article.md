## Overview

Real-world EV range in Africa diverges significantly from manufacturer-stated range figures, and every dealer needs to understand exactly why. The spec sheet number is produced under controlled test conditions that do not reflect African operating realities: altitude, temperature, road conditions, payload, and driving habits all reduce effective range — some of them substantially. Dealers who set accurate range expectations at the point of sale prevent post-purchase complaints. Dealers who fail to do this generate warranty disputes, negative word of mouth, and damaged reputations. This guide explains the gap, quantifies it, and gives you the language to communicate it honestly and confidently.

---

## Where the Spec Sheet Number Comes From

Chinese EVs sold for export are typically rated under the WLTP (Worldwide Harmonised Light Vehicles Test Procedure) standard. WLTP testing is conducted:

- At sea level (0m altitude)
- At 23°C ambient temperature
- With no cargo or passenger payload beyond the driver
- On a smooth test track surface
- With HVAC switched off for a portion of the test cycle
- At moderate, controlled speeds

This is a standardised laboratory approximation — it allows fair comparison between vehicles, but it is not a real-world driving simulation.

The gap between WLTP rated range and real-world range in typical use is **15–25%** globally, according to testing by transport research organisations. In African operating conditions, the gap is larger.

## The Six Factors Reducing Range in Africa

### 1. Altitude (significant)

At Addis Ababa's elevation of approximately 2,400m, air density is about 76% of sea level. This affects EVs in one primary way: the HVAC system — especially the air conditioning compressor — works harder in thinner air to move the same thermal energy. AC draw is one of the largest energy consumers in an EV after the motor itself.

**Range reduction from altitude effect on HVAC: 5–10% in Addis Ababa.**

### 2. Temperature (significant at the cold extreme)

Ethiopian highland mornings are cold. Addis Ababa regularly sees temperatures below 10°C before 7am. Cold temperatures affect EV range in two ways:
- Battery chemistry is less efficient at low temperature — internal resistance increases, reducing available energy
- Cabin heating draws directly from the battery (EVs have no engine waste heat to redirect)

Resistive heating (common in lower-cost Chinese EVs that lack heat pump systems) consumes 2–5 kWh/hour. At highway speed, this alone represents a 10–20% range reduction.

**Range reduction from cold morning temperature + heating: 10–20% in Addis highland winters (October–February).**

### 3. Road surface and gradient (moderate)

Unpaved roads increase rolling resistance versus the smooth test track surface of WLTP testing. Gravel roads in typical Ethiopian conditions add approximately 5–8% energy consumption per kilometre compared to sealed tarmac.

Gradient is also significant: the road from Addis Ababa to Hawassa descends 700m over approximately 250 km — efficient (regen braking recovers energy on descent). The return journey climbs 700m — energy intensive. Hill driving on highland routes can affect per-journey range significantly.

**Range reduction from unpaved roads: 5–8%.**

### 4. Speed

EV energy consumption increases exponentially with speed. WLTP testing includes mixed speed cycles. Sustained highway driving at 100–120 km/h (common on the Addis–Adama expressway) consumes significantly more energy per kilometre than urban driving.

| Driving speed | Relative energy consumption |
|---|---|
| Urban stop-go (30–50 km/h average) | Low — regen braking recovers meaningful energy |
| Mixed driving (60–80 km/h) | WLTP baseline |
| Highway (100–120 km/h) | 20–35% more than WLTP |

### 5. Payload and passenger load

WLTP testing uses driver only. A fully loaded 5-seat vehicle (driver + 4 passengers + luggage) adds 350–500 kg over the test condition. More mass = more energy to accelerate = more range reduction. A fully loaded taxi or family trip vehicle loses 5–10% range compared to the test figure.

### 6. HVAC usage pattern

Air conditioning in East African climates (lowland regions, daytime in Addis from March to May) operates continuously. A running AC compressor draws 1–3 kW continuously — at 60 km/h, this is equivalent to 1.7–5 kWh per 100 km of additional consumption. On a 60 kWh battery pack, continuous AC at 3 kW represents roughly 5% of usable capacity per hour.

## The Combined Effect: African Range Reality Table

The following table shows approximate real-world range expectations for a vehicle rated at 400 km WLTP, under different African operating scenarios:

| Scenario | Approx. range reduction | Expected real-world range |
|---|---|---|
| Urban Addis Ababa, mild day, no AC | –15% | ~340 km |
| Urban Addis Ababa, cold morning, heater on | –25% | ~300 km |
| Highway driving 100 km/h, lowland Ethiopia | –30% | ~280 km |
| Fully loaded (5 persons + luggage), highway | –35% | ~260 km |
| Mixed driving, Addis–Hawassa return (gradient) | –20% | ~320 km |

*Editorial estimate. Actual range varies by model, battery chemistry, vehicle weight, and tyre condition.*

## How to Communicate Range to Customers at the Point of Sale

The worst thing a dealer can do is quote the WLTP figure without context. When the customer finds out (and they will) that real range is significantly less, they feel misled — even if they cannot articulate exactly why.

**The correct approach: set the adjusted figure upfront.**

**Script for a customer asking about range:**

"The manufacturer rates this vehicle at [X] km. In Addis, with our altitude and typical driving, most owners see [X × 0.80–0.85] km in practice — that's around [adjusted figure] km. That's still more than enough for [customer's stated usage]. And if you're doing a long highway trip, plan on [X × 0.75] km to be safe."

**If the customer pushes back on the lower number:**

"The same gap exists with petrol cars — the manufacturer's fuel consumption figure is always better than what you see in real driving. This is just how all vehicle ratings work. What I can tell you is that the real-world range covers your typical driving day with margin."

## Range Anxiety vs Range Reality: Two Different Problems

**Range anxiety** is a psychological phenomenon — the fear of running out of charge before reaching a destination, even when there is objectively enough range to complete the journey.

**Range reality** is an operational planning issue — the vehicle's actual range under local conditions must exceed the intended journey distance with a safety margin.

For most urban and peri-urban Ethiopian buyers (daily commute under 80 km), a 400 km WLTP vehicle offers 280–340 km of real-world range — 3–4 days of driving before a charge is needed. Range anxiety is unfounded; range reality is manageable.

For intercity use (Addis to Dire Dawa: ~510 km by road), a 400 km WLTP vehicle is genuinely not adequate for a single-charge journey. This is not a deficiency to hide — it is a use case the customer needs to understand before purchase. Recommend they verify charging availability en route before committing to intercity routes.
