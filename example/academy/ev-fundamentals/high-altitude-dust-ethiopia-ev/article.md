## Overview

Understanding how high altitude and dust affect EVs in Ethiopia is essential knowledge for any dealer or technician operating in the East African market. Addis Ababa sits at approximately 2,400 metres above sea level — making it one of the highest capital cities in the world — and much of the country's road network crosses unpaved terrain where fine particulate matter is a constant operational reality. Neither of these factors will prevent EVs from working well. But ignoring them leads to avoidable failures, warranty disputes, and customer complaints that damage your reputation. This guide explains the specific mechanisms at work and gives you concrete steps to manage them.

---

## Why Altitude Matters for Electric Vehicles

The widely repeated claim that EVs "don't care about altitude" is partially true and mostly misleading. Unlike internal combustion engines, electric motors do not require oxygen for combustion — so you will not see the 10–15% power loss that ICE vehicles suffer above 2,000m. However, altitude creates three real effects that dealers and technicians must understand.

**1. Air cooling efficiency drops**

Most Chinese EVs sold in Africa use air-cooled or passively ventilated battery packs. At 2,400m, air density is roughly 76% of sea-level density. This means the cooling airflow passing through or around the battery pack carries less heat per unit volume. Under sustained load — climbing a long gradient, running at highway speed, or fast-charging repeatedly — thermal management is less effective than the manufacturer's sea-level specifications assume.

Practical implication: on sustained highland climbs, battery temperature may rise faster than the BMS expects. Well-designed BMS units will throttle output to protect the pack. Less sophisticated BMS implementations in lower-cost models may not respond quickly enough. Know which category your stock falls into before selling into high-altitude routes.

**2. Regenerative braking output is slightly reduced**

Regen braking works by running the motor as a generator. At altitude, slightly lower air resistance and reduced rolling resistance (thinner air = marginally lower drag) means the regen capture opportunity is also marginally lower. This effect is small (1–3%) and rarely operationally significant, but it is worth knowing when customers ask why their regen "feels different" after driving up from lower elevations.

**3. HVAC system works harder**

Air conditioning and cabin heating systems operate on refrigerant cycles, which are affected by ambient air density. In thin air, the compressor works harder to move the same amount of thermal energy. This increases energy draw from the battery. At Addis Ababa's altitude combined with its cold mornings (temperatures frequently below 10°C at dawn), heater demand in particular can reduce effective range by 15–20% compared to manufacturer range figures tested at sea level.

## Altitude and Fast Charging

Fast charging (DC charging at 30kW+) is the primary context where altitude becomes a serious concern in Ethiopia.

Fast charging generates significant heat inside the battery pack. At sea level, the thermal management system — whether active liquid cooling or passive air cooling — is rated to handle this heat load. At 2,400m, the reduced air cooling efficiency described above applies directly.

**What this means in practice:**

| Charging scenario | Sea level | 2,400m (Addis) |
|---|---|---|
| 30kW session, 20–80% SOC | Normal | Monitor for thermal throttling after 40 min |
| 60kW session, multiple sessions back-to-back | Normal | High risk of BMS thermal limit trigger |
| Overnight slow charge (7kW AC) | Normal | Unaffected — low heat generation |

*Editorial estimate — specific thresholds vary by model and BMS implementation.*

**Recommendation for dealers:** For customers operating fast-charge-dependent fleets in Addis (taxis, ride-hail), advise spacing fast charges with at least 30-minute cooling intervals between sessions. Overnight AC charging should be the primary charging method wherever possible. This advice protects battery longevity and reduces the likelihood of warranty-related disputes about premature capacity fade.

## How Dust Affects EVs: Three Entry Points

Ethiopia's road network is approximately 85% unpaved outside major urban centres, according to Ethiopian Roads Authority data. Even in Addis Ababa, construction activity and dry-season dust are persistent. Dust affects EVs through three distinct entry points.

**Entry Point 1: Cooling inlets and radiators**

Despite having no combustion engine, EVs still have cooling systems for the battery, inverter, and motor. These systems typically use air inlets — often located at the front bumper or undercarriage. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10 dust common in Ethiopian highland and lowland regions) accumulates in these inlets, reducing airflow and insulating surfaces against heat dissipation.

**Maintenance action:** Inspect and clean cooling inlets every 5,000 km or monthly during dry season, whichever comes first. Use compressed air at low pressure (do not use high-pressure water near electrical components). If your workshop is near construction sites or unpaved roads, increase this to every 3,000 km.

**Entry Point 2: Cabin air filters and HVAC**

The cabin air filter in most Chinese EVs is a standard replaceable cartridge — identical in concept to ICE vehicle filters. In Ethiopian dust conditions, these filters clog 2–3x faster than in the vehicle's design market (typically China or Europe). A clogged cabin filter forces the HVAC blower motor to work harder, increases energy draw, and reduces air quality for occupants.

**Maintenance action:** Replace cabin air filters every 10,000 km in Ethiopian conditions, versus the 20,000–30,000 km interval specified in most owner manuals. Stock filters in your spare parts supply — this is a high-turnover consumable.

**Entry Point 3: Connector contacts and charging ports**

Dust accumulation inside the charging port is an underappreciated failure mode. When dust-contaminated plugs are inserted repeatedly, the abrasive particles score the contact surfaces inside the port. Over time, this increases contact resistance, which causes heating during charging and — in severe cases — intermittent charging failures or damaged connectors.

**Maintenance action:** Install a rubber cap on the charging port when the vehicle is not charging. Clean the port with a dry cloth or very low-pressure compressed air before each charging session in dusty environments. Do not use liquid cleaners inside the port.

## What Dealers Should Tell Customers at the Point of Sale

Setting accurate expectations at handover prevents warranty disputes and repeat complaints. The following talking points are based on the operational realities described above.

**On range:** "The manufacturer range figure is tested at sea level in moderate temperature. In Addis, with altitude and HVAC use, expect 80–85% of that figure in typical daily driving. This is normal and not a defect."

**On charging:** "Prefer slow overnight charging when possible. If you use fast charging regularly, do not stack multiple fast-charge sessions back-to-back. Allow the car to cool between sessions."

**On maintenance intervals:** "In Ethiopian conditions, cabin filters need replacing more often than the owner manual states. We will remind you at your 10,000 km service. Keep the charging port capped when not in use."

**On warranty coverage:** Altitude and dust-related wear (scored contacts, clogged filters, accelerated thermal cycling) fall into a grey zone in most Chinese OEM warranty terms, which are written for home-market conditions. Managing these proactively means fewer borderline warranty conversations.

## Identifying Altitude and Dust-Related Faults

When a customer brings in an EV with a complaint, the following fault patterns should prompt you to investigate altitude and dust as contributing factors.

| Symptom | Likely cause | First check |
|---|---|---|
| Range significantly below expectation | HVAC draw at altitude, degraded cabin filter | HVAC usage pattern, filter condition |
| BMS thermal warning during fast charge | Reduced cooling at altitude | Cooling inlet blockage, charging session pattern |
| Charging port won't engage or fails mid-session | Contact scoring from dust | Port condition, connector contact surfaces |
| HVAC blower noise increased | Clogged cabin filter forcing motor to work hard | Filter condition |
| Battery capacity fade within 18 months | Repeated thermal stress from back-to-back fast charging at altitude | Charging log review via OBD or BMS data |

## Summary: The Addis Checklist

If you operate or service EVs in Addis Ababa or other highland Ethiopian locations, print this and put it in your workshop.

- [ ] Set customer range expectations to 80–85% of rated figure on handover
- [ ] Advise slow overnight charging as primary method; limit back-to-back fast charging
- [ ] Inspect and clean cooling inlets every 5,000 km (dry season: every 3,000 km)
- [ ] Replace cabin air filters every 10,000 km (not manufacturer interval)
- [ ] Fit rubber cap on charging port; clean before every fast-charge session
- [ ] Know whether your models use active liquid cooling or passive air cooling — the former handles altitude better
- [ ] Document altitude-related customer advisories given at handover — this protects you in warranty conversations
