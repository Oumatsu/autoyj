## Overview

EV objections from African customers follow predictable patterns. After thousands of sales conversations across East and West African markets, the same five concerns surface repeatedly: battery life, charging infrastructure, spare parts, resale value, and price premium. Knowing that these objections are coming — and having a structured, credible response ready for each — is the core skill that separates dealers who convert EV enquiries from those who lose them to ICE alternatives. This guide gives you the framework, the language, and the numbers.

---

## Why African EV Objections Are Different

Before the scripts, understand the context. African customers buying EVs are often making a higher-stakes decision than customers in mature markets. They may have limited access to warranty enforcement, fewer financing options, less peer experience to draw from, and genuine concerns about infrastructure that are not hypothetical — grid reliability in East Africa is a real operational variable.

This means your objection handling cannot be dismissive or generic. "Don't worry about that" is the worst possible response. What works is acknowledging the legitimate concern, providing a specific and honest answer, and then redirecting to the genuine advantages.

---

## Objection 1: "What happens when the battery dies?"

**What the customer means:** They are worried about catastrophic battery failure requiring full replacement — a cost they assume will be unpayable. They may also be conflating "battery degradation" with "battery failure."

**The honest answer:** Modern lithium battery packs in Chinese EVs are designed for 1,000–1,500 full charge cycles before reaching 80% of original capacity. At typical urban driving distances in Addis Ababa or Nairobi (40–60 km/day), this translates to 8–12 years of daily use before the pack noticeably underperforms. A full battery replacement is expensive (typically 30–40% of vehicle cost), but this scenario is rare within the working life of most fleet or personal vehicles.

**The redirect:** "The battery is designed to last longer than most buyers keep a car. What affects battery health most is how you charge it — and we will walk you through that before you drive off the lot. For the first 3–5 years, the risk of battery failure is extremely low."

**What to avoid:** Do not quote a specific replacement cost unless you have confirmed your local supplier's current pricing. Costs vary by model and market. Do not promise specific warranty terms unless you have the written documentation to back them up.

---

## Objection 2: "Where will I charge it? The grid here is unreliable."

**What the customer means:** This is often the most legitimate objection on the list, because it is grounded in real operational experience. Power outages in Ethiopia average several hours per week in some districts, according to Ethiopian Electric Power reporting. Customers have seen phones and laptops fail mid-charge; they reasonably extrapolate this concern to a vehicle.

**The honest answer:** Home charging (overnight, on a standard 220V socket with a slow charger or the vehicle's portable EVSE) is the resilient model for African EV ownership. The vehicle charges slowly over 8–10 hours, which means a brief grid interruption of 1–2 hours simply means slightly less charge by morning — not a failed charge session. Fast charging is more sensitive to grid stability, but most personal vehicle owners do not depend on fast charging for daily use.

**The redirect:** "The way most people charge in Addis is overnight at home on a slow charger — similar to how you charge your phone. A short power cut just means you get 90% charge instead of 100%. For your typical daily driving, that is still plenty of range."

**Supporting point:** If the customer has access to solar at their home or business, an EV is actually more resilient than a petrol vehicle in a fuel shortage — a scenario that East Africa has seen multiple times in recent years.

---

## Objection 3: "What if something breaks? Where do I get parts?"

**What the customer means:** This is a supply chain and maintenance access objection. The customer has likely seen an imported car from an obscure brand become a permanent workshop visitor after a single component failure with no local replacement available.

**The honest answer:** This is a legitimate concern, and your answer should be differentiated based on the brand. For high-volume Chinese brands — BYD, Chery, MG, SAIC — local parts networks in East Africa are building out rapidly. Ethiopia has seen formal BYD service points established in Addis Ababa. For smaller or newer brands, parts sourcing is genuinely more complex, and being honest about this builds trust.

**The redirect:** "For the brands we stock, we maintain a local buffer of high-turnover consumables — cabin filters, brake pads, tyres, and common sensors. For more complex components, we have established supplier relationships with lead times of 2–4 weeks from China. The parts that break most often on EVs are actually simpler than on petrol cars — there's no gearbox, no clutch, no exhaust system. The common faults are manageable."

**What to avoid:** Do not name parts suppliers or quote specific lead times unless you have confirmed relationships. A promise of "2-week delivery" that becomes 8 weeks will cost you the customer's trust permanently.

---

## Objection 4: "Will I be able to sell it when I want to upgrade?"

**What the customer means:** Resale value risk is high in the mind of any rational buyer in a market where EVs are still rare. If they cannot sell it, they are stuck with it.

**The honest answer:** EV resale markets in East Africa are early-stage. The honest position is that used EV values are currently less predictable than used ICE values, because the secondary market is thinner. However, this is changing. BYD and MG used vehicles are transacting in Addis Ababa and Nairobi's used car markets. The trajectory is toward more liquidity, not less.

**The redirect:** "Resale is improving as more EVs come onto the road and buyers become familiar with them. The vehicles that hold value best are from brands with growing local presence — BYD and MG are the clearest examples right now. What we do know is that EVs have significantly lower running costs than petrol cars, so the total cost of ownership over 3–5 years is usually lower even if resale is uncertain."

**The TCO bridge:** Move the conversation from resale speculation to running cost certainty. A Birr-per-kilometre calculation using local electricity costs versus petrol prices is concrete and compelling. This is covered in depth in the TCO Conversation Guide (Sales Playbook).

## Objection 5: "It costs more than a petrol car of the same size."

**What the customer means:** The customer is comparing the sticker price of an EV to an equivalent ICE vehicle and seeing a gap. This is often true at the purchase price level, especially for quality EVs versus basic ICE alternatives.

**The honest answer:** The purchase price gap is real. The correct response is to acknowledge it and then reframe the conversation around total cost of ownership over the customer's expected holding period. Do not pretend the EV is cheaper to buy. It often is not, at least not yet at the entry level.

**The TCO redirect:**

| Cost component | ICE equivalent | EV (Addis Ababa) |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel/electricity per 100km | ~ETB 450–600 (petrol) | ~ETB 80–120 (electricity) |
| Engine oil changes per year | 3–4 x ETB 2,000–3,000 | None |
| Gearbox, clutch, exhaust maintenance | Significant over 5 years | Not applicable |
| Brake pad wear | Standard | Reduced (regen braking extends life) |

*Fuel and electricity costs based on Ethiopian Energy Authority and petroleum retail pricing as of Q1 2026. Exchange rates fluctuate — verify current ETB costs before quoting customers.*

**The language:** "The vehicle costs more today, yes. But let me show you what the monthly running cost difference looks like over the years you plan to own it. For most buyers we talk to, the total cost is lower even if the upfront price is higher."

## The Meta-Skill: Objection Sequencing

Most EV sales conversations do not end on one objection. A customer who raises the battery concern will often follow with the parts concern, then the resale concern. Managing the sequence matters.

**Recommended order to address unprompted:**
1. Lead with running costs (turn the price objection pre-emptively)
2. Address charging and grid concerns early — it is the objection that kills the most deals
3. Let battery life, parts, and resale come from the customer and answer them as they arise

Do not volunteer every potential concern before the customer raises it. Answer what they ask, and answer it honestly and specifically.

---

## Closing After Objections

Once you have addressed an objection, do not immediately move to the next topic. Confirm the answer landed. A simple: "Does that answer your concern about the battery?" before moving on prevents the customer from mentally revisiting the same objection later in the conversation.

After all raised objections have been addressed: "Based on what we've discussed, it sounds like the [model name] would fit your situation well. Would you like to do a test drive today so you can experience how it feels to drive?"

The test drive is your best closing tool. An EV's driving experience — quiet, responsive, no gear changes — almost always makes a positive impression. Get them in the seat.
