## Overview

Kenya has been quietly building the most coherent EV policy framework in Sub-Saharan Africa. While other nations make dramatic announcements and then reverse course, Kenya has taken an incremental, infrastructure-first approach — and in early 2026, formalised that approach with a dedicated **National Electric Mobility Policy**.

For dealers, Kenya already offered the most straightforward import economics on the continent: **0% import duty on electric vehicles**, with no equivalent exemption for petrol imports. The new policy adds a layer of institutional support that signals long-term market stability.

---

## What Kenya Already Had: The Foundation

Before the 2026 policy, Kenya's EV incentive stack was already the strongest in East Africa:

- **0% import duty** on fully electric vehicles (confirmed and maintained)
- **Nairobi** and **Mombasa** — the two key ports of entry — with established customs processes for EV classification
- A functioning ride-hailing sector (Uber, Bolt, Little) actively trialling EV fleets
- A growing base of commercial importers sourcing directly from Chinese manufacturers

The 0% duty position means Kenya's landed cost advantage over markets like South Africa (25% duty) or Ethiopia (20% duty) is significant. A $20,000 vehicle that costs $24,000 to land in Addis Ababa arrives in Nairobi for $20,000 in duty terms alone.

## What Kenya's Emerging 2026 Mobility Policy Framework Signals

> **Editorial note:** Kenya has been developing a formal National Electric Mobility Policy through 2025–2026. Several provisions below are reported by EV industry media as under active development or gazettement. Dealers should verify the current formal status of specific measures with the Kenya Revenue Authority or Kenya's Ministry of Transport before relying on them for compliance planning.

Kenya's evolving policy direction — as reported by EV24.Africa and the Africa E-Mobility Alliance — points toward four additional measures beyond the established 0% duty:

### 1. Green Number Plates (Reported as Under Development)
A proposed distinct green registration plate for EVs would enable data collection on fleet size and build consumer visibility. The measure signals the government's intent to track and incentivise EV adoption as a distinct category.

### 2. Tax Benefits for Commercial EV Operators (Direction Confirmed)
The policy direction clearly prioritises commercial fleet electrification — taxi companies, ride-hailing platforms, and logistics firms operating EV fleets above a minimum threshold are the target beneficiary group. Specific incentive rates are subject to ongoing gazettement.

### 3. Charging Infrastructure Requirements for Dealers (Proposed)
A proposed obligation would require importers above a volume threshold to either operate or partner with certified charging point providers — targeting grey-market traders who import without any support infrastructure. **Verify current enforcement status with KRA before structuring your dealer registration.**

### 4. EV Classification Standards (Direction Confirmed)
Kenya is moving toward formal technical standards that define what qualifies for the 0% duty rate. Mild hybrids and 48V micro-hybrid systems that do not meet a minimum pure-electric range threshold would not qualify — closing a loophole that some importers have been exploiting.

The confirmed, bankable element remains the **0% import duty on full BEVs** — this has been in place and consistently enforced. The additional framework elements above represent the direction of travel; their implementation timeline should be verified directly with KRA.

## The East Africa Opportunity

Kenya's policy matters beyond its own borders. As a founding member of the East African Community (EAC), Kenya's regulatory direction tends to influence its neighbours — Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Burundi — over time. Rwanda already has full EV tax exemptions. Tanzania is watching closely.

A dealer who establishes a strong position in the Kenyan market — with proper OEM relationships, after-sales infrastructure, and compliance with the 2026 policy requirements — is building a platform for regional expansion as the EAC harmonises its EV standards.

The Mombasa port, which handles a significant proportion of East Africa's automotive imports, is also the gateway for landlocked markets like Uganda, Rwanda, and eastern DRC. Landing vehicles in Kenya and distributing regionally is an established commercial model.

## What Dealers Should Do Now

- **Register vehicles correctly**: ensure your customs agent applies the correct HS code for full BEV classification — the green plate benefit and duty exemption both depend on this
- **Start the after-sales infrastructure conversation**: the new policy's charging requirements will tighten over time; early compliance signals professionalism to both regulators and commercial fleet buyers
- **Target the ride-hailing sector**: Nairobi's Uber/Bolt fleet is actively replacing ICE vehicles; fleet deals of 10–50 units are realistic for credentialled dealers with proper documentation
